An agent-driven workflow for personalized mRNA cancer vaccine design

Abstract

Personalized neoantigen cancer vaccines aim to direct immune responses toward mutant peptides that are present in a tumour but absent from healthy tissue. Early clinical studies of individualized RNA vaccines have shown that this is a viable therapeutic strategy, while also underscoring the practical challenge of selecting the right targets and producing a suitable construct for each patient [1,2]. This project presents an agent-driven workflow that links these computational tasks in a traceable sequence: somatic-variant analysis, neoantigen prioritization, coding-sequence design, untranslated-region selection, and manufacturing-oriented quality control. Rather than replacing established scientific tools, the system brings them together in one pipeline that automates the computational components of vaccine research. Ultimately, it illustrates how AI agents can coordinate complex, multi-step workflows in therapeutic development.

Why this matters

The promise of a personalized cancer vaccine is clear: a tumour’s mutations can create peptide targets that are displayed on the cell surface by MHC molecules and absent from healthy tissue — a handle the patient’s own immune response can be directed at. Further, there is a scalable strategy of identifying mutations through modern genomic analyses. The difficulty is that most mutations will not lead to peptides that are useful vaccine targets. They must be expressed, processed, presented by the patient’s MHC molecules, and recognized by T cells. Large datasets and modern prediction studies have improved our ability to predict whether mutations will lead to potential therapeutic targets, however, much work is still needed in order to bring these insights together into a single pipeline [3,4].

Once suitable epitopes have been identified, they must be combined into an mRNA construct suitable for therapeutic administration. This requires a sequence that can be synthesized, delivered, translated, and assessed for sequence-level risks. Again, modern workflows address parts of this design problem, but efforts are scattered. Codon choice and RNA structure are coupled, so optimizing one in isolation is not a defensible design strategy [9,10].

Using a suite of AI agents, this project addresses the complete computational workflow, from epitope discovery to mRNA construct design.

What the workflow does

The pipeline consists of five stages. The first derives annotated somatic variants and HLA context from genomic input. The second ranks candidate neoantigens using peptide–MHC binding, immunogenicity, processing, expression, clonality, and related evidence where available. The third encodes selected peptides into a coding sequence and searches the trade-off between codon adaptation, GC content, uridine content, and RNA folding. The fourth selects and evaluates 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions. The final stage scans the assembled construct for defined sequence hazards and records advisory measurements such as degradation and predicted IVT yield.

Each stage preserves its inputs, outputs, and the scores that informed a decision. When models make different predictions, their results are retained rather than hidden in a single number. This is important because prediction tools can disagree on individual peptides even when they perform well on aggregate benchmarks [5,6].

Benchmarks

Genomic entry

The first stage was evaluated on five melanoma cases from the Hugo 2016 cohort, using a real FASTQ-to-variant path and published calls as the reference. Mean somatic-SNV recall was 0.870 (range, 0.846–0.888). OptiType reached a mean four-digit HLA accuracy of 0.967 — exact for four of the five cases, 0.833 for the fifth — with two-digit typing correct for all five. Both are a promising baseline [7,8].

Neoantigen ranking

The most relevant benchmark asks a hard question: among peptides that already look like binders, which should receive attention first? On a Müller 2023 contrast of 171 immunogenic peptides and 1,780 hard negatives, the project’s combined ranking returned an AUROC of 0.834 and an AUPRC of 0.449. In the unadjusted split, binding alone accounted for much of the discrimination. After caliper matching on binding, binding-only performance fell to near chance, while the immunogenicity component remained predictive. This supports the use of multi-signal prioritization, while also showing that ranking metrics depend critically on the choice of negative examples [3,4].

Construct design and quality control

The complete five-stage chain has been run end to end on a real human melanoma case (Müller 2023 Patient 9; supplied HLA-A02:01/A30:02/B07:02/B08:01/C07:01/C07:02), with live backends at every stage: NetMHCpan 4.1, MHCflurry and BigMHC for ranking, AlphaFold-Multimer co-folding of the peptide–MHC complexes, a rigorous-Lagrangian and DNAChisel coding-sequence ensemble, UTR-LM for untranslated-region scoring, and a three-backend degradation panel with ESMFold folding of the assembled polyepitope. From 60 somatic mutations and 73 ranked candidates the workflow produced a 1,791-nt coding sequence and a 2,095-nt mRNA candidate with CleanCap AG and m1Ψ-ready chemistry, an estimated IVT yield of 78.4% and a clean splice-consensus scan. Codon adaptation and several RNA-structure metrics were retained as advisory criteria because mRNA design requires a balance between stability and translation, not optimization of one metric alone [9,10].

That candidate was not cleared for manufacture: the final scan found two Type IIS (BbsI/BpiI) restriction sites, at positions 698 and 1,061, and the hard gate rejected it. This is the intended behaviour rather than a failed run. The coding-sequence stage had not been constrained to avoid Golden-Gate sites, and the manufacturing stage caught what it missed — a design/manufacturability mismatch that is now being closed at the design stage. Reporting the rejection is the point; a workflow that only ever reports success is not performing quality control.

The workflow is not specific to human input. The same five stages have also been run to completion on a canine osteosarcoma case, using canine DLA alleles and a canine codon table, producing a 3,176-nt construct (66-nt 5′ and 1,199-nt 3′ untranslated regions, 20 peptide cassettes) that passed every hard gate. Species-dependent resources — codon usage, MHC alleles, reference proteome and expression references — are configuration rather than code, so the same pipeline serves both.

The final QC layer uses more than one degradation-related signal, including thermodynamic and learned predictors. These estimates are useful for comparison and triage, but they are not evidence of efficacy and do not currently block a construct. RNA degradation models vary substantially in their relationship to experimental measurements and are best treated as complementary, not definitive, predictors [11–13].

Conclusion

This project demonstrates how AI agents can coordinate the many computational steps of personalized mRNA vaccine research in one transparent workflow. From genomic analysis to mRNA construct design and quality control, the pipeline connects established tools and makes it possible to follow how a vaccine candidate was selected and designed.

The benchmark results indicate that the approach is promising for variant recovery and neoantigen ranking, while the end-to-end case studies show the workflow can carry a real patient’s mutations through to an assembled, fully instrumented mRNA candidate — including, in the human case, correctly refusing to clear one. The next steps are experimental: verifying selected peptides, assessing RNA expression and stability, and testing generated candidates in preclinical and clinical settings. By making the computational workflow more automated and traceable, this project aims to support researchers developing personalized mRNA cancer vaccines.
