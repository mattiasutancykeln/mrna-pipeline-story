2.1 · Translation

The mutated gene is transcribed and translated into protein — the mutation rides along.

2.2 · Processing

The cell shreds a sample of its own proteins into short peptides, 8–11 amino acids long.

2.3 · Presentation

One fragment is clamped in the groove of an MHC molecule and carried to the surface — the display case.

2.4 · Recognition

A T cell patrols. If the peptide looks foreign — because it carries the mutation — the cell is flagged.

3.1 · Ranking

Dozens of mutations were found. One composite score ranks them — binding, expression, clonality, foreignness — so the pipeline spends its payload on the few most likely to provoke a response.

3.2 · Selection

The top candidates become the vaccine's cargo. Our mutation — a strong, clonal, expressed binder — rises to the top.

4.1 · Reverse translation

Each chosen peptide is written back into RNA. Every amino acid has several synonymous codons; the optimiser picks the ones the ribosome reads fastest and most stably.

4.2 · Assembly

The epitopes are strung into one reading frame, flanked by untranslated regions that tune how often the message is read, then finished with a 5′ cap and a poly-A tail.

4.3 · Folding

The finished message is folded in silico — a stable, well-formed structure survives longer in the cell and is translated more reliably.

5.1 · Hazard scan

Before manufacture the sequence is swept for trouble: restriction sites that would cut it, degradation-prone stretches, toxic translation products.

5.2 · Modification

Uridine is swapped for N1-methylpseudouridine — the chemical tweak that lets an mRNA vaccine slip past the innate immune system.

5.3 · Clearance

Every gate passes. The construct is manufacturable — a synthesisable sequence, designed end-to-end from a single mutation.
